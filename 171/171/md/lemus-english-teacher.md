# My English Teacher

![](el-maestro-de-ingles-2.png){class="a"}

## By Claudia Lemus

Frank Smith was my English teacher at the school that was founded back in 1968 at 368 Ituzaingó Street, San Isidro, Buenos Aires. Today, that address no longer exists; the building is now part of the courthouse complex.

I remember that he had a very distinctive way of speaking, rolling his “r”s and switching the articles on nouns. This fascinated me, because it felt like a kind of rebellion against the rules of grammar. Ever since I was a little girl, I’d thought that the language had been made more complicated than it needed to be. Otherwise, why “la vaca” and “el toro”? It could just as easily be “la vaca y el vaco.” Or, if they preferred, “el toro y la tora.” The same thing happened with the toad and other animals. But the teacher broke those rules.

I learned several things from him, but to be honest, not much English. A little bit of pronunciation, because we sang, but I didn’t even know what I was saying. But I have to say that now I understand English quite well.

For example, from him I learned that chickens don’t have four legs—they have two.

One day, he had assigned us homework to draw several chickens—I don’t remember how many, but around seven. And I drew them with four legs each, which was normal for me.
In the next class, feeling very proud of my work, I opened my notebook to show the drawing; I was sitting in the front row, right in front of the blackboard (and next to Bibi, the teacher’s daughter and my friend).

Frank came over and started laughing hysterically, as if he’d run out of breath, and when he could finally speak, he asked me:

> > *Claudia, how many legs do chickens have?*

I looked at him very seriously and replied, “Four.”

More laughter from the teacher, and then he said: - “But they have two legs!”

I looked at the drawing and remarked:“No wonder I didn’t have room to draw that many legs!” (I’d drawn them like an open fan).

And since I didn’t feel like redrawing the whole thing, Frank told me I could fix it by covering up two legs on each chicken and drawing grass around them.

As I write this, I laugh too, but mostly because I’m remembering the teacher’s laugh. Both he and his daughter Bibi had such hearty laughs that they were music to the soul.

He also taught me that a teacher—a man—could cry in public and show his emotions.

On April 4, 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated, and Frank came into the classroom with reddened eyes; he said nothing and began writing Martin Luther King’s name on the blackboard, while weeping inconsolably in silence. I watched him write with his back to us, his lines slanting downward. And he told us that a great person had died. It struck me to see him crying so bitterly, and I thought that Martin Luther King must have truly been a great person to cause my teacher such sadness.

We didn’t have class that day.

I also learned from Frank that if you cut yourself (specifically while shaving), you could put little pieces of toilet paper on the wound to stop the bleeding. Apparently, handling a razor wasn’t the English teacher’s strong suit. He’d often show up with his face looking like a “collage” of little pieces of white paper, and I found that image very funny.

Frank knew how to play a few songs on the piano at school while we students sang along. I didn’t really know what he was saying, but I repeated what he told me to, and I imagined it was a scene from a movie.

The teacher would sometimes lose his patience and yell at a classmate, but he never yelled at me. And the truth is, I don’t really know what caused him to lose his patience—probably because I was lost in my own fantasy world, distracted (which happened to me very often).

When I was about ten years old, my father was very sick and couldn’t work. At home, they told me I wouldn’t be able to go to the Waldorf school anymore because they couldn’t afford the tuition. My mother went to the school to explain the situation. Later, she told me that Mr. Frank had said, “The school needs Claudia’s presence more than the money her tuition represents.”

Hearing that the teacher had said this made me feel that I could be a valuable person. And that helped me a great deal.

That’s how I continued attending classes on a scholarship—I don’t know for how long.

Many years later, in 1985, when I traveled to Germany with the intention of training to become a Waldorf teacher, Frank helped me again. By chance, I managed to slip a note into an envelope addressed to him from when he lived in Switzerland. He contacted some of his acquaintances so that my husband Francisco and I could get jobs as student assistants at the seminary in Stuttgart.

In any case, we returned to Argentina shortly afterward because my father was diagnosed with a terminal illness.

It was a very difficult time for me in every way…

But there was Frank again. He found jobs for Francisco and me, something we still remember with gratitude.

Today, my former English teacher is a well-known figure in the anthroposophical community. And I’m proud to have been his student.

I hope you remember me, Frank.
