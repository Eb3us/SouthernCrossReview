# Reorganizing the Civilized World - Volume One

![](reorganizing-civilized-world-1-main.jpg){class="a"}

## by Rudolf Steiner

**Stuttgart, April 22, 1919**

In accordance with the program of tonight's meeting, it will be my task
this evening to speak about the Appeal "To the German People and the
Civilized World\", which you have in your hands. You will allow me to
speak more aphoristically today when speaking to a meeting of people who
are essentially familiar with the appeal. Next Monday I will speak more
directly about the social views on which the appeal and my book on the
social question, which will be published in a few days' time, are based.

The motives for such an Appeal, which is a compassionate impulse for
humanity, have nothing to do with programmatic ideas supporting this or
that interest. No, I am speaking of facts that speak loudly and clearly,
which have arisen out of the terrible catastrophe \[World War I\] which
we have endured in recent years. If you look at these facts attentively
and alertly, you will come to a very specific impression. I would like
to characterize this impression in the following way.

It has often been said that the horror we have gone through during the
years of this worldwide catastrophe is without precedent in the whole
period of time we call history. Shouldn't we evoke something else now,
something which, it seems to me, has not yet been truly evoked: the
feeling that it is now necessary for a worldwide reorganization of
conditions that are emerging from radically new human impulses that not
only break radically with old institutions, but also break with old
habits of thought. Should we not, by looking at the facts that speak so
strongly, say to ourselves that shadows are spreading over large parts
of the civilized world that have been left over chaotically from
previous humanity to present humanity.

Can we say, on the other hand, that out of the confusion and chaos such
thoughts have already emerged? Does it not seem to us, if we look at
these facts clearly, that the old political opinions are there, old
social views are there, certain ideas about how things should be among
people are there, but all that is not enough to reorganize what remains
from the immediate past into our present.

This poses great, comprehensive challenges for the present. We can
perhaps do justice to them most easily if we ask ourselves openly and
honestly how we got into these circumstances. If I were to describe the
most significant phenomenon of the present and ask, for example, what
has been the cause, I cannot point out that it has resulted from the
aberrations of one or another class of people. I would rather say that
what is actually happening today is bursting forth as if from an abyss.
It is an abyss that has opened up in the course of the last three to
four centuries between the hitherto leading classes of humanity and
those who are striving to emerge from being led and are today raising
their demands. The turmoil does not come from one side or the other, but
from what lies in between.

This is not a pedantic remark, it is something that I believe can be
substantiated and at the same time sheds light on what actually needs to
happen. On the one hand, we have the leading circles of humanity, who --
let us all admit it openly and honestly -- have developed in the course
of the last centuries, and especially the last century, in such a way
that they have shown little inclination to look into the future, to have
any idea of what may actually live in the heart of the social order
within which they live.

When we look at what has happened under the influence of the thoughts,
feelings, will and actions of the hitherto leading circles of humanity,
then we recall the degree of insight, the degree of clear thinking that
existed in the spring of 1914. It is necessary to point out such things
today. In the Spring of 1914, we heard that at a meeting of the men who
were supposed to be enlightened, at least with regard to political
matters, and to whom the leadership of the people was entrusted at that
time, the then Foreign Minister said that he could inform the gentlemen
of the German Reichstag that the general détente in Europe was making
great progress. The relations of the German Empire with Russia were the
most satisfactory imaginable, for the St. Petersburg government was not
at all inclined to listen to the hounding of the press; the friendly and
neighborly relations between the German Empire and Russia promised to be
the very best. He also said that negotiations had been initiated with
England, which had not yet been concluded, but which promised that the
best relations with England would be achieved. Indeed, if one wants to
consider openly and honestly what the leading circles, and those
selected from these leading circles, thought at that decisive time, then
one must point out such things. The above was said in the weeks
immediately preceding that terrible time in which, at least ten to
twelve million people were killed and three times as many crippled in
Europe! These things must be looked at, because it is important to
finally move away from what in recent times has been called reality and
gain confidence in what real insight into the facts is capable of. If we
do not decide to look courageously at what we have been led to by
thoughtlessness about what the present indicates for the future, then we
cannot move forward. That is what must be considered today. I really
don't want to say anything personal to you this evening, but perhaps I
may point out one thing by way of introduction.

At the same time that the leaders were talking about "general
relaxation" and the like, as I have just mentioned, I summarized in a
small meeting in Vienna an idea I had formulated over decades about the
future possibilities of European, and of modern civilized life in
general. I had to say it to a small group; a larger one would probably
have laughed at me, because all those who had the leadership of mankind
in their hands at that time only regarded such things as fantasy. I put
what I had to say into the following words, merely repeating what I had
already said in one form or another over the past decades: The
prevailing tendencies in the present will become stronger and stronger
until they finally destroy themselves. By means of spiritual insight
into society, one will see how terrible tendencies towards social
ulcerations are sprouting up everywhere. This is the great cultural
concern for those who can observe such existence. This is the dreadful
thing that has such an oppressive effect, and which, even if one could
suppress enthusiasm for the recognition of the processes of life by the
means of a spiritual science, would impel him to speak of the remedy, to
cry out to the world the remedy for what is already so forcefully
approaching, and which will become ever stronger and stronger. What
nature creates through abundance becomes cancerous when it enters social
culture in the manner described.

It seems to me that these words are a more accurate reflection of what
followed the spring of 1914, when they were spoken, than all the words
spoken by those who considered themselves practical, who believed that
they were drawing from realities, while they were only drawing from
their political illusions of life.

To describe briefly what has led to such things, it is precisely the
lack of foresight, of wanting to foresee what lies in the womb of the
present as the germs of the future. I'm not to accusing, merely
characterizing. Look at what has been accomplished in the last few
centuries by the so-called bourgeois leading class; we must say that
much has been achieved that is extraordinarily praiseworthy, much that
cannot be described in any other way than as enormous progress in
general of human culture up to the present day.

But what have these advances and the resulting confusion made necessary?
We needed a certain broadening of education when modern technology with
its necessary accessories of modern capitalism developed on the one
hand, and on the other hand the modern worldview, which runs parallel to
capitalist and technical development. I will say something very
paradoxical, but the truths that are necessary for us today may still
sound somewhat paradoxical to the thinking habits of the time. Among
those who have expressed themselves well, I know of only one man who has
said in the right way how the world should actually be treated if things
were to continue as they have been in these leading circles for
centuries; one man who has said what these leading circles would have to
do if they were consistent. And this man -- and this is the paradox --
is the head of the Holy Synod, as it is called in Russia, the Chief
Procurator Pobjedonoszev. There is a writing by this man which, in an
extraordinarily forceful and witty way, radically condemns the
parliamentarism of modern times, as well as democracy, but above all the
press of the western world. Pobjedonoszev was foresighted enough to know
that either these things -- parliamentarism, the press, democracy --
would have to be done away with, or we would destroy what the leading
circles believe to be the right thing for modern times. Of course, only
such a chairman of the Holy Synod had the courage to speak in such a
radical way: that what was in the minds of the people who thought most
progressively, in the leading circles, was a contradiction.

It was basically contradictory to the invention of the art of printing.
It was impossible to use all the newer inventions and institutions to
call on the people to judge for themselves, to think intelligently, and
at the same time continue doing business in the same way as before. This
was bound to lead to what it did: the self-destruction of this culture.
If the statements of the chief procurator Pobjedonoszev had been known
in the widest circles, then one would have said to oneself long ago:
something else, something radically different is necessary than what we
have allowed to emerge in the last centuries. I say this without
accusation, just to characterize it. One could see from the statements
of the Chief Procurator, even if they are of course nonsense for more
recent times, that a radical change was necessary. For one could only
have adhered to it if one thought like he did.

That is the paradox, which must be said on the one hand. That stands on
one side of the abyss. Then comes the abyss, and on the other side stand
the rising proletarians, those who have been called in the course of the
last centuries to the machine, to the factories, to soul-desolating
modern capitalism. Out of their souls arose those demands which today
are not merely questions of bread; they are that too -- but the
important thing today is not the question of bread, for that is
basically justified for all people in Central Europe -- but it is, as we
shall see in a moment, a comprehensive economic, rights and
spiritual/cultural question.

But let us now look at the other side of the abyss from the point of
view of that very side. Let us look at what is arising in the
proletarian world. Truly, it was something significant to witness what
was developing there. While on the one hand the bourgeois circles formed
the upper class and shaped a certain culture that could only develop
when built on the substructure of the proletariat, while the upper class
of the bourgeoisie, the middle- class, developed its own culture. One
could see how for decades the little time that the proletarian had to
spare after his work had been occupied with a certain social view of the
world and of life. This grew out of completely different foundations
than bourgeois culture. You only know what that means if you have
learned to think not only about the proletariat, but to think with the
proletariat. That is what is important today in order to evaluate this
side of the abyss.

And what do we see on this side? Well, there are already areas of the
hitherto civilized world where the proletariat is called upon to create
order out of chaos. We have seen it develop, by the ingenuity that
corresponds to the unspent intellect of the proletariat. we have seen
the idea of the social worldview of the proletariat endowed with
tremendous power. We have seen it develop up to the outbreak of the
World War catastrophe. We know how comprehensive views have developed
about what should happen. Now many who have grasped these ideas in their
own way, who believe that they have understood the proletarian
worldview, now stand in such a position that they could carry out this
worldview. And now certain institutions have been handed over to them in
large areas of Europe.

We see on the one hand a worldview that is in decline, and on the other
hand a certain human current that has not been able to find the social
impulses that can lead to a reorganization. Between the two lies the
abyss, and out of this abyss surges what is already beating against us
today and which will truly beat ever more strongly against humanity,
both bourgeois and proletarian, if this humanity is not able to
understand what the vital necessities of human development in the
present and the near future are. These vital necessities are visible
when one observes the proletarian movement as it is emerging, when one
sees how it has gradually developed.

What lives in the proletarian soul develops in three spheres of life,
including an inevitable demand to be satisfied in the present and the
near future. Those who have become somewhat familiar with the
proletarian worldview and life, which has been repeatedly summarized by
insightful people with the words: "It cannot go on like this", found how
deeply an idea has penetrated the proletarian thinking of recent times.
It emanated from the proletarian leader whose name has lived on in the
European and American proletariat for seventy years, and who, despite
all his successors, has not yet been surpassed: Karl Marx. One need only
know how everything connected with the expression "surplus value" has
impressed the thinking of those people who, worn out by work, want to
enlighten themselves in their evening meetings about what is going to
happen.

This touched the deepest feelings of the proletariat. But it not only
touched the deepest feelings of the proletariat. At the same time, it
touched the most intimate demands of modern humanity's development. But
if one really wants to understand such things, one must look deeper than
what people say with their reason, with their head-consciousness. In the
depths of the human soul there often lies something quite different from
what people know how to consciously make clear to themselves. Infinitely
meaningful things were stirred up in the proletarian soul when "surplus
value" was spoken of, something that the proletarian has no clearly
conscious idea of, but which lives in him and which is now breaking out
with elementary force, and which must be understood if one wants to find
a way out of the turmoil. Whether the doctrine of "surplus value" in the
sense of Karl Marx is acceptable in the judgment of economic science is
not important for what is meant. Even if this idea were based on error,
its socially agitating effect in the working class would have to be
considered a historical phenomenon.

What was actually living in the depths of the proletarian soul when
"surplus value" was spoken of? Well, the leading class spoke of human
development; they felt themselves engaged in this human development.
When they wanted to express what actually underlies this human
development, they said, according to the situation, divine world
government, moral world order, historical ideas, or the like. The
proletarian, who in the dawn of this newer age had adopted this
bourgeois worldview as an inheritance, was offered certain concepts that
had been developed in the course of time. But he could see nothing when
he looked at the leading circles, of what these leading circles spoke of
as divine world guidance, moral world order and historical ideas.

Why couldn't he see anything? Well, he was overworked, but he was not
overworked by any moral world order or divine world order, but by the
yoke of the new economic order. And he looked at what was developing as
culture among the leading classes. What did he perceive there? He felt
the only relationship he really had -- for he could not have any other
-- to this culture, to this cultural heritage of the leading classes.
His relationship was that he worked for what this culture demanded, he
produced "added value" for others, that's what he understood.

And what was offered to him of this cultural heritage in all kinds of
popular entertainment, popular theater performances, popular courses,
and artistic popular performances of other kinds, were things he could
not relate to. For one can only do so if one is socially active in the
corresponding culture. But the abyss between the two classes had opened
up. But one thing came up that cut deeply into the heart of the
culturally minded if he took part in proletarian life and proletarian
striving. It came about that within the proletariat the soul-destroying
view took root that spiritual-intellectual life, art, religion, custom,
law, science are all basically nothing but the reflection of economic
life.

Among the insightful proletarians one word could be heard again and
again to describe cultural life: ideology. What the proletarian felt
when he looked at art, at modern science, at religion, custom and law,
was for him nothing other than something that rises like smoke from
economics, the only material reality: ideology. And the view that cut so
deeply into the heart was the view that perceived all spiritual life,
the entire content of the human spirit, as ideology. One can
theoretically have this view, as did the modern proletarians, especially
their leaders: All spiritual-cultural life basically springs from the
unreal human thoughts caused by the conditions of economic life. One can
even prove this view strictly scientifically. Oh, what cannot be proved
strictly scientifically! We have learned much of this in recent times.
Of course, this view can be proven scientifically. But one thing cannot
be done with this view: it cannot be lived with. And the tragic irony of
recent times is that the proletarian placed trust in the bourgeois
social class by taking over what has become of bourgeois culture, and it
was perceived as an empty web of thoughts, like smoke rising from
economic conditions. But one can only live with cultural life if one's
soul is deeply affected by it. Otherwise, the soul becomes desolate,
empty.

And no one understands the terrible cultural damage who does not know
that precisely under this apparently so easily provable concept of the
ideology of spiritual life that the soul had to become desolate, and
that it therefore despaired of anything else in life than at most an
improvement of material conditions. That is the essence of the actual
spiritual demands of the modern proletariat. It cannot be characterized
in any other way than by saying that the bourgeois society of recent
times has handed down to the proletariat a culture which cannot ennoble
the soul and spirit of man; and now this bourgeois society is confronted
by what has become of the desolate souls, of the souls left empty. They
were summoned to participate in the education that had to be spread in a
democracy.

One could not exclude them, and of course did not want to. But if you
were a member of the bourgeois social class, you still lived in the
impulses from old religious ideas, from old moral or aesthetic views
from olden times. The proletarian was placed next to the machine, was
crammed into the factory, into capitalism. This gave him nothing that
could answer the big question: What am I actually worth as a human
being? He could only turn to the scientific orientation of modern times.
Cultural, intellectual life became an ideology for him, something that
desolated his soul. This gave rise to his still undefined demands. Only
an understanding of this fact can lead to a beneficial path to the
future. Things are much more serious and in a completely different area
than is usually believed.

The proletarian, for his part, has now gradually come to see quite
clearly how, in more recent times, intellectual culture derived from the
economy of bourgeois circles. How people were placed, how their
existence and their economic circumstances were, so was their spiritual
life. In recounting these things, I may perhaps refer to a personal
experience, because I consider this personal experience to be
extraordinarily characteristic. For many years I was a teacher of the
most diverse branches of human knowledge at the workers' training school
founded by Wilhelm Liebknecht.

I also taught rhetoric. In my dealings with the students who are now
involved in party politics, I was able to study much of what had
developed around the turn of the nineteenth century to the beginning of
the twentieth century. At that time, when I lectured on history, I tried
to make it clear to my students what made intellectual life an ideology,
and that is precisely the economic conditions of the last four
centuries. As the proletarian theorist essentially limits himself to the
observations of life in the last four centuries, he comes to regard the
whole of intellectual- cultural life as ideology. But it has actually
only become so in the last four centuries.

The proletarian worldview commits the error of thinking that a fact of
the past four centuries is also a fact of the whole development of
humanity. I have said again and again that this is true for the past
four centuries, but we are now faced with the challenge of replacing
ideology with a real cultural-spiritual life that sustains the human
soul. Salvation lies not in the realization that spiritual-cultural life
is ideology, but in the will to re-create a spiritual-cultural life that
is not ideology. For this ideology is the inheritance of bourgeois
society. Eventually I was pushed out of the school by the political
party leaders, even though the students themselves were in my favor and
understood me. It was not so easy to gain understanding for those ideas
which must be the fundamental ideas of a social reorganization, if one
first looks at the social question as a spiritual-cultural question.

The second thing we see as the area of life from which the proletarian
demands have developed is the area of rights, the area which, as the
appeal says, is meant to be the responsibility of the state. What then
are actually rights? I have made an effort over the decades to
understand people's different views on the concept of rights. I must
affirm that when you consider what you understand by rights in a
realistic way, that is not theoretical, you end up saying to yourself:
Human rights are something that is an elementary element in every
healthy human breast. Just as the ability to see blue or red as a color
comes from the healthy eye, and just as one can never teach someone who
has a diseased or blind eye the concept of the blue or red color, so one
cannot teach anyone what rights are in any concrete area unless the
consciousness of rights, which is something elementary, something
primordial, just as seeing color or hearing sound is something
elementary, lives in him.

Consciousness of rights springs from a different aspect of the soul than
everything else in the development of mankind. What is otherwise created
in culture is based on talent. Consciousness of rights, however, has
nothing to do with talent or aptitude. It is something that essentially
develops in human nature, but only in dealing with people, just as
language can only be learned in contact with people. This consciousness
of rights, whether it speaks loudly and clearly or whether it springs
darkly in the human soul, is something that the human soul wants to
develop within itself. When the proletarian took part in modern
educational conditions, through democracy, the question of rights also
arose in him. But what did he find when he asked about rights?

Look into his soul and you will find the answer to this question. He
found, when he judged the meaning of rights, not rights, but privileges
conditioned by the difference of the classes. He found that what had
become established as rights had actually only meant privileges for the
favored class, and nothing for the propertyless classes. He found class
struggle in the law instead of the exercise of rights. This convinced
him that he could only advance if he was a class-conscious proletarian,
if he sought his rights from within this class. This led him to the
second element of his worldview. The need to overcome class differences
so that the structure of the rule of the rights-state could emerge from
these class differences, which have arisen in the course of historical
development.

The third area, from which arose those proletarian demands, which are
also necessary demands of the present, is the economic area. This
economic domain, as it has emerged so clearly through the capitalist
world order and through modern technology -- how did it affect the
proletarian? How did this economic order, this economic cycle, affect
the proletarian? He saw himself completely caught up in this economic
cycle. The others, they had culture and intellectual life, which he
regarded as an ideology and in which participation was actually a lie
for him, because he was not part of the social context from which it had
sprung. The bourgeois circles had their special privileges and cultural
assets, and they had an economy that accompanied them. For them, life
was divided into three parts, even if they summarized it in the unitary
state. But he, the workingman, felt that his whole personality was tied
up in this economic life. How so?

This, in turn, can be answered by looking at his feelings, which have
developed ever more strongly in the course of the last six to seven
decades. Just as the proletarian realized that he had nothing of the
cultural-spiritual domain, that he had no other relation to it than that
he was allowed to produce surplus value for it, he also naturally had
the feeling that there is something in this economic domain which must
not be in it if he, as a proletarian, wanted to get a humane answer to
this very question: What is human life worth in the world context?

Essentially, only what can be described as goods or human labor moves in
the economic cycle. The production of goods, the circulation of goods,
the consumption of goods, that is basically what the economic cycle is
all about. It was this for the leading circles, but for the proletarian
it was different. His labor power was woven into this economic cycle.
Just as goods were bought on the commodity market, human labor was
bought from the proletarian. Just as the commodity had its price, human
labor in the form of wages had its price on the labor market. This again
is something that touched the unconscious feelings of the proletarian
soul, something that did not necessarily come to full consciousness, but
which was lived out in the undeniable facts of the present.

When Karl Marx uttered the words "labor as a commodity". The
proletarian, looking back, stood within the historical development of
mankind by understanding these words about the commodity of labor power
in his own sense. In ancient times the economy needed slaves. The whole
human being was sold like a commodity or like an animal. Later came
serfdom. Less of the human being was sold, but still a lot. Then came
the newer era, when, in order to develop capitalism, the masses of the
proletariat had to be educated in a certain way, which had to cultivate
democracy in a certain way. And it was not understood at the right time
to see what was in the present as a seed for the future. It was not
observed at the right time how necessary it is to forcefully separate
the buying and selling of human labor from the economic cycle.

The modern proletarian perceived the fact that he had to sell his
labor-power on the labor market according to supply and demand, as one
buys and sells goods, as a continuation of the old slavery. In this way,
he felt that he was weaved into the economic process and did not feel
that he stood out from it. He felt completely integrated into it.
Because if you have to sell your labor-power, you sell the whole person,
because you have to carry the whole person to where you sell the
labor-power. The time had come when it should have been realized that
human labor-power had to be integrated into the social organism in such
a way that it was not a commodity, and where the old wages system could
no longer exist. That was overlooked. That is the tragedy of the
bourgeois view of life, that everywhere the right moment that was
necessary in the development of modern capitalism and democracy was
missed.

This is what has ultimately caused the current chaos, not from below by
the proletarians, but from a failure by the bourgeoisie to understand
the times. "My fault, my grievous fault", the leading circles should say
to themselves, for this feeling would flow a clear sense of what
actually must happen. This characterizes what is now blazing up from the
abyss as a threefold demand -- as a spiritual demand, a rights demand
and an economic demand. And we must not continue to insist on the error
that salvation can come from the economic sector. For that is precisely
the bad thing, the harmful thing, that the modern proletarians have
become completely enslaved to the economic system. They must get out of
the economic system!

I could only give a sketch of what has developed historically. Whoever
follows these things as they have developed in the course of recent
times with an insightful eye; whoever has the good will and the inner
sincerity and honesty to look beyond economic, historical and other
opinions to what is real, will come to the necessity of this threefold
structure of which the appeal speaks, precisely from what has developed
in this way over time, by observing the circumstances of the last three
to four decades in particular.

The proletarians have only seen that intellectual culture is dependent
on economic life. From this they formed the idea that all cultural life
must be dependent on economic life. They could not discern the fact that
this intellectual-culture, due to its inner weakness, and to the fact
that it no longer had the impetus of the old worldviews, had condemned
itself to be an appendage of economic life. They had paid less attention
to something else, which for the reason mentioned above has also
remained unseen by the bourgeois: that intellectual culture has also
become dependent on the political state. I would like to consider the
historical justification of this dependence in modern times as something
necessary.

But it is also necessary to consider the right time for this
cultural-spiritual life to be emancipated, not only from economic life,
but also from the political state. In the course of the last four
centuries, intellectual culture of the civilized world has become more
and more dependent on the political state. This has almost been regarded
as progress. Certainly, it was necessary in order to liberate cultural
life from the shackles of the Church; but now this is no longer
necessary. It was seen as progress to place spiritual culture entirely
under the wing of the political state. What it has come to is that
culture is dependent on the needs of the state, which is turn has become
dependent on economic needs -- which are not generally human needs, as
the World War catastrophe has clearly shown. The war has made us
particularly aware of this in Germany because of certain events, which I
would call symptomatic. I could multiply the symptoms a hundredfold,
even a thousandfold, but you will understand me if I point out something
studied during the war itself, which brought everything to an extreme.

But the matter has always been there. A very important scientist of the
recent past, for whom I a have the greatest respect, said something that
is particularly indicative of the dependence of science on the modern
state. He spoke as Secretary General of the Berlin Academy of Sciences,
which he called "The scientific protection force of the Hohenzollerns".
With regard to mathematics and chemistry, the relevant fact is very much
concealed, but it is there too. But if you go to those areas that touch
on a major vital question of worldview, to the field of history, in
recent times intellectual life has truly become nothing other than
scientific protection for the modern state. But spiritual culture cannot
be cultivated in its inner essence by passing laws on freedom of
teaching, on free science and free teaching. Laws have no influence at
all on spiritual-cultural life, which depends on basic human capacities.

Anyone who looks into the connection between human and individual
capacities and society knows that intellectual life can only prove
itself in reality if it is compelled to prove this reality by means of
its own nature, if it is left to its own devices from the lowest school
up to the universities, rather than being an appendage of the state.
Until now, Social Democracy has only been able to demand things based on
certain sentiments: Religion must be a private matter. In a similar way,
all cultural-intellectual life must become a private matter in relation
to the political state and economic order if it wants to prove its own
reality. This reality can only be proved if this cultural-intellectual
life is left to its own devices.

One will have to realize the monstrosity that results in the fact that a
party based merely on intellectualism, such as the Center, has crept
into a parliament such as the German Reichstag, where only human rights
and the like should be formulated. The moment such a party enters the
political state, this state is necessarily weighted on one side. In the
state only that in which all men are equal can flourish, just as they
are equal to a certain extent in language. Within the state, only that
can flourish which is not based on special human talent, but on what is
determined from person to person out of the original consciousness of
rights.

The demand to completely separate culture as a separate organization
arises from an understanding of cultural life and from an understanding
of the conditions that have arisen in recent times from the intertwining
of cultural life with the state. There is no need for the socialists to
fear that, for example, the unified school, which is demanded by them,
could be endangered by placing even the lowest level school in an
independent cultural-educational administration. The conditions of
social life will be such in the future that it will not be possible to
create special schools for the different social classes. Especially if
the teachers are not civil servants, but are only dependent on a
cultural administration, then nothing other than the unified school will
be able to exist. For how did the estates come into being? Precisely by
the fact that culture became intertwined with politics.

On the other hand, economics must be detached from politics. By making
such a demand, one is all the more deeply involved in practical life.
For economic activity, as it has developed in modern times, has
something so arbitrarily compelling that it has moved beyond the
outdated ideas of the political state and other concepts. However,
people today still do not think much about this because they do not look
at what the necessary demands of modern times are. Let me give you a
concrete example, an example which could be multiplied a hundredfold,
and which shows how economic life has emancipated itself from other
areas, from culture and rights, in modern human development.

I would like to refer to the necessary extraction of pig iron in the
early sixties of the nineteenth century. At that time the German iron
industry needed around 799,000 tons of pig iron, which was extracted by
just over 20,000 workers. In the relatively short time up to the end of
the eighties, the German iron industry needed 4,500,000 tons of pig iron
compared to the previous 799,000 tons. These 4,500,000 tons of pig iron
were mined by roughly the same number of workers. What does that mean?
It means that regardless of everything else that has happened in the
development of mankind, regardless of what has taken place in the
development of mankind, with 20,000 people purely through technical
improvements, at the end of the eighties about five times more iron was
extracted than in the sixties.

In other words, the technical-economic sphere has become independent,
has stood out from the rest of human development. But no attention was
paid to it, it was not even seen -- and this example could be multiplied
a hundredfold -- how economic life emancipated itself. Nowhere has it
been observed what has been done in the economic field itself, the
progress that has been made in economic activity through technology. Do
not forget the opinion expressed here. Technology has advanced, but that
technological progress should be accompanied by the corresponding social
progress has been ignored. He who knows how to observe facts knows that
this modern economy has emancipated itself, and that if one demands this
same emancipation by the political state, one demands nothing more than
that people should agree to it and make the necessary arrangements.
Thus, the necessity for the emancipation of economic life is what the
facts demand. But what will the consequences be?

A fundamental requirement of modern life can only be fulfilled by
separating economic activity from the political state. In this respect,
development must proceed in the opposite direction to that thought by
some socialist thinkers of recent times. Whereas many socialists believe
that economics must develop as if in a great cooperative, that it must
also embrace cultural life and the state, it is precisely the opposite
-- economic activity must be only be responsible for the cycle of
commodity production, commodity circulation, commodity consumption. That
is what alone can lead to the satisfaction of the necessary demands of
present-day life. You see, on the one hand, economic life depends on the
conditions of nature. We can only master natural conditions to a certain
extent. Whether an area is fertile, whether the soil contains raw
materials for industry, whether there are fertile or unfertile years,
these are natural conditions; they are a foundation of economic life.

That is the foundation on one side. On the other side it must in future
be supported by something else, which can be regulated just as little by
economic activity as the natural force in the soil. After all, you can't
make regulations about the forces of nature. On the other hand, economic
life must border on the legality of the state. So just as economic life
borders on one side the conditions of nature, on the other side it must
border on the legality of the state. This includes property relations,
labor relations, labor law. Today the situation is such that the worker
is still tied into the cycle of economic activity with his labor. Labor
must be separated from the cycle of economic life, despite Walther
Rathenau's fears.

And it must be organized in such a way that the amount, time and nature
of labor are determined democratically by the state, which is completely
independent of economic activity. The worker will then, before he begins
industrial economic activity, have himself co-determined the amount,
time and nature of his work. The basic character of economic activity is
to produce goods so they can be consumed. And the very nature of
economic activity is that everything used within it is inevitably
consumed. If human labor is used within the economic process, then it is
consumed. But human labor power must not be consumed, it must not be a
commodity. It must be determined legally by a political state that is
independent of economic life, just as the forces of nature, which are
independent of the economic cycle, determine it in the earth. Before the
laborer begins to work, he has legally determined the nature, amount and
time of his work.

I know all the objections that can be raised against what has been said.
One thing above all can be objected to. As a necessary consequence of
this view, what is called national prosperity would be is dependent on
labor law. Yes, that will happen, but it will be a healthy dependence.
It will be such a dependence that does not ask about producing and
producing and producing again and again, but that asks: How does the
human being, who must participate in the economic process, keep himself
healthy in body and soul despite the economic process? How, in addition
to the consumption of labor power, can he be assured of rest from work
so that he can participate in the general culture, which must become a
general human culture, not a culture dependent on social classes?

For this he needs rest from work. And only when it is recognized that
this rest from work is just as much a part of work, of social life, as
labor power, then we will emerge from the turmoil and chaos of the
present. It is necessary that those for whom the above is like biting
into a sour apple should bite into it. Otherwise, they will realize in a
completely different way what the modern demands mean, which do not
spring from human souls alone or from human minds, but from the
historical development of humanity itself.

For once this demand concerning labor law is fulfilled, pricing will be
dependent on labor law in a healthy way and not the other way around, as
it still is today despite some labor protection legislation. The wage,
that is, the price of human labor, will depend on other conditions. The
human being will determine what can be achieved in economic life.
However, we will have to be reasonable in a certain direction in the
determination of labor law and property relations, just as we have to be
reasonable towards nature, which can only be dealt with to a limited
extent through technical facilities.

But on the whole, economic life must be interwoven between legal rights
and the conditions of nature. This economic activity must be organized
on purely economic forces, on associations which will be formed partly
from the professions, but especially from a harmony of consumption and
production. For lack of time,

I cannot go into the causes of the great economic crises, especially not
into how they ultimately led to the great catastrophe, the Baghdad
Railway and the like, but it is necessary to consider how these things
must actually be thought of.

You see, a healthy economic life can only result if consumption is
regarded as the decisive factor, not production. Now I may perhaps
mention something that was once attempted, which only failed because
such a single attempt must fail within the entire old economic order. It
can only succeed when the economic order is radically emancipated from
the other aspects. In a society that most of you do not love very much,
because it has been much maligned, we tried, before the catastrophe of
war approached, in the business of food production, to accomplish some
of the things that must be developed in order to become the economic
order of the future.

As a society, we were able to provide consumers. The consumers were
there first, and it was a matter of producing according to the needs of
consumption. For various reasons it failed, but especially because of
the war catastrophe, when such things were not possible. But take
another example, which may seem strange to you, because in contrast to
the "idealism" of the present, it unjustifiably conflates culture with
economics for many; the idealists of materialism are strange people. In
the same Society, which, as I said, many of you will not love, I have
always tried to place the economic element of cultural production on a
sound basis. Just think about the often-unhealthy basis, economically
speaking, of today's cultural production.

It is an example for what should not prevail even in the broadest areas
of economic activity. A book is printed in an edition of a thousand
copies. There really are quite a lot of books today that are printed in
such numbers, but about fifty of them are sold, the others are thrown
away. What has actually happened when 950 books are thrown away? So many
typesetters, so many bookbinders have worked unproductively, work has
been done for which there was not the slightest need. I believed that
within this Society, which rightly or wrongly many of you do not love,
the necessity has arisen to establish a book publisher in which a book
is only published when it is certain to find a buyer, where only as many
copies are produced as there is a need for, so that human labor is not
fragmented into nothingness by typesetters and bookbinders, but where
what is created is adapted to human needs. And that is what must happen,
that production must be adapted to needs. But this can only happen if
economic activity is arranged on the basis of associations in the manner
described.

Since the eighteenth century, the threefold motto: liberty, equality,
fraternity has resounded in modern society. Who has not heard these
three words ringing in the human heart in such a way that he knows that
they mean something great? But there were clever people in the course of
the nineteenth century who considered that these three human impulses
contradict each other. They really do contradict each other. Three great
human impulses contradict each other. How so? Because they were devised
at a time when people felt real human impulses, but they were still
hypnotized by the unitary state. People could not yet accept the fact
that the salvation of the future could only lie in the threefold
society, in a spiritual-cultural organism, an economic organism and a
rights-state organism. So, it was believed that freedom, equality and
fraternity could be realized in a unified state.

They contradict each other. If you divide the healthy social organism
into its three natural parts, then you have the solution to what the
human soul has been pining for over a century: Freedom is the basic
impulse of spiritual-cultural life, where the freedom of individual
human abilities must be built upon. Equality is the basic impulse of the
political rights-state, where awareness of the equality of human rights
must exist. Fraternity is what must prevail on a large scale in the
economic sphere of life; this fraternity will develop from the
Associations. Suddenly these three words take on a meaning, an
unexpected meaning, when one drops the prejudice of the unitary state
and becomes convinced of the need for the threefold structure.

I can only hint at all these things, and I realize that many people
still say today: these things seem incomprehensible to me. I have tried
again and again to find the reason for the incomprehension in the
Appeal. And there were many among those who said that they found it
incomprehensible who had understood what they had been ordered to
understand during the past four and a half years \[the war years\].
People have understood some things that I really didn't understand. But
with this Appeal something reaches the human mind that it should
understand out of its free inner resolution. However, this requires
inner strength of the soul. But this inner strength of the soul will be
needed if we want to get out of the chaos and confusion of this time.

The Appeal was first attempted during the terrible times in which we
found ourselves. It was first intended as the basis for a foreign
policy, for I assumed that if the ideas of this Appeal had been given a
certain amount of life, it would have been possible for them to resound
in the thunder of the cannons. Then something would have flowed from
Central Europe which could have resounded out into the world in such a
way that it would have been equal to Woodrow Wilson's so-called Fourteen
Points. These fourteen points, which are conceived in completely
different interests than the Central European ones, should have been
countered by Central European interests. Then it would have been
possible to speak of understanding, whereas all other talk of
understanding was hollow. That is what was tried first when it could
have had an effect. But it was like preaching to deaf ears. Those people
who still had influence at that time, those who were the successors of
those who spoke of the "progress of general détente" just before the
murder of ten to twelve million people, were told: you have the choice
of either accepting reason now or expecting ruin later.

What is written in this appeal, as I said at a decisive moment in 1917,
is not the brainchild of one person, but has arisen from devoted
observation of the developmental needs of Central and Eastern Europe.
You have the choice of either placing what needs to be realized before
humanity using reason, so that this humanity of Central Europe has a
goal again and can speak of it, or you are faced with the most terrible
cataclysms and revolutions. In those days people listened to such
things, they understood them, but they did not have the will, or rather,
they did not find the bridge from intellectual understanding to the
development of the will. Today the facts speak loudly that the bridge
must be found from understanding to will. That is what is to be said to
mankind through this Appeal. It is to be understood from free inner
determination. It is to be understood with the will to think.

I will do what I can to contribute to this through the book "Die
Kernpunkte der sozialen Frage" \[Toward a Threefold Society - *Basic
Issues of the Social Question*\], which is being published now. But
humanity will have to acknowledge that completely new ways of thinking
are necessary for the new organization, that something is needed that
has not been thought of as being on the left or the right. These things
should not be taken lightly. Humanity will be forced by the facts to
recognize that the time is over when people were told: you can only be
happy, content and socially viable if the throne and altar are in order.
A different song is ringing out from the east of Europe today: "Throne
and altar" are to be replaced by "office and factory". In what underlies
the office and the factory, lies something quite similar to that which
underlies the throne and the altar.

Only if we are comfortable looking neither to the left nor to the right,
but only at the great historical necessities of development, will we
find the way by which we arrive at what we need, namely, at nothing
extra-human, neither at throne and altar nor at office and factory, but
at the liberated human being. For by structuring the social organism
into three parts, you allow man to participate in all three. He
participates in economic life, he participates in the democratic state,
he participates in spiritual- culture, or has a certain relationship to
it. He will not be fragmented but will be the connecting link between
the three areas. It will not be a question of resurrecting the old class
distinctions, but precisely of overcoming the old class distinctions, of
a completely free human being, by the social organism itself structuring
the outer life of man in a healthy way.

That is what it will be about in the future. We can only liberate man,
we can only place him on his own, if we place him in the world in such a
way that he participates in all three areas without his humanity
becoming fragmented. You can see, however, how these things are still
quite difficult to understand today. I recently gave a lecture on these
things in a town in Switzerland. A speaker stood up and said that he
didn't really understand the threefold structure, because justice could
only develop based on the state, it would also have to permeate culture
and economics, so justice would have to be developed in all three areas.
I replied with a comparison to make the point clear. I said: Let's
assume that a rural family community consisted of the husband, the wife,
children, maidservants, farmhands and three cows. The whole family needs
milk to live, but it is not necessary for the whole family to produce
milk; if the three cows produce milk, the whole family will have milk.
Thus, justice will prevail in all three areas of the social organism if
justice is legally produced in the emancipated state. It is a matter of
returning from clever thoughts and ideas to simple thoughts and ideas of
reality. I am convinced that this Appeal is not understood because
people do not take it simply enough. Those who take it simply will see
how it and its ideas speak of a longing to gradually emerge from the
turmoil and chaos of the present, from the trials of the present, to a
life in which the uniformly healthy human being, the mentally,
physically and spiritually healthy human being, can develop precisely
through the threefold structure of the social organism.

Translated by Frank Thomas Smith
